The story before the chain

The Baseline Timeline

Creation to the Temples, in order, start to finish — the whole arc the wall poster is drawn on. Read it straight through; it ends by handing you off to the Chain of Torah.

1
בְּרֵאשִׁית
Creation
the beginning

God makes the world — light, then sky, then land, then plants and animals. People come at the very end, made on purpose and placed in charge of taking care of everything that came before them.

Why do you think people come last in the story, not first?
2
נֹחַ
Noach & the Noachide Laws
covenant #1

After the flood, God makes a promise — a brit — never to flood the whole earth again. It comes with a short list of basic rules meant for everyone, not just Jews: don't murder, don't steal, treat animals fairly.

If you could only pick a few rules that everybody in the world had to follow, what would you pick?

This is the first promise God makes, and it's addressed to all of humanity. Hold onto the shape of it, because the same thing happens twice more — and each time it gets more specific.

3
אַבְרָהָם
Avraham
covenant #2

Avraham looks around at a world full of idols and works out, on his own, that there's actually one God behind all of it — the first person in the story to really get that. God answers with a new promise, narrower than the last one: this family, specifically, is going to matter, and become a nation of its own.

What do you think it felt like to be the only person who believed something everyone else thought was crazy?
4
אָבוֹת
The Patriarchs
three generations

The promise gets handed down rather than repeated from scratch: Avraham to Yitzchak, Yitzchak to Yaakov, and Yaakov to his twelve sons — who become the twelve tribes.

Why do you think the Torah spends so much time on just one family's story instead of a whole nation right away?
5
אֶרֶץ כְּנַעַן
The Promise of Canaan
unpaid, for now

Part of what God promised Avraham was land — this specific place, Canaan, would belong to his descendants. That part of the promise does not get paid off for a very long time.

How many generations do you think pass between the promise and the family actually living there?
6
יְצִיאַת מִצְרַיִם
Egypt & Passover
~1313 BCE

The family grows into a nation while living in Egypt — and then gets enslaved there for generations. God sends Moshe; ten plagues follow; the people go free, and the sea splits on the way out.

We've now had a flood story and a sea-splitting story — what do you think water keeps showing up to represent?
7
מַתַּן תּוֹרָה
Wandering & Sinai
covenant #3

At Sinai, God gives the Torah — and this time not to one family, but to the entire nation, all at once, out loud. It's the biggest version yet of the same pattern you've been watching: everyone, then one family, then a whole people — in a real relationship, with real terms.

Why do you think God waited until after they were already free to give them the rules, instead of before?

This is the exact moment the Chain of Torah page picks up — right here, at Sinai.

8
מִדְבָּר
The Hard Part
forty years

It does not go smoothly after that. There's a golden calf, and then a moment where the people are too frightened to enter the land they were promised — and are told they'll have to wait. Forty years of wandering follow.

Is it harder to trust a promise before you've seen it come true, or after you've already seen God do the impossible and you still mess up anyway?
9
מֵי מְרִיבָה
Moshe's Punishment
year forty

At Mei Meriva, God tells Moshe to speak to a rock and it will give water. Instead Moshe loses his temper — "shall we bring water out of this rock for you?" — and hits it. Twice. The water comes anyway, but Moshe is told he will not be the one to lead the people into the land after all.

What was the actual problem — hitting the rock instead of speaking to it, losing his temper, or making it sound like his own power rather than God's? Does it have to be just one?
Three answers Rashi says the miracle got downgraded — a rock that talks is a bigger sign than a rock that's hit. Rambam says the anger itself was the problem. Ramban points at the words: "shall we bring water" made it sound like Moshe and Aharon's own power, and said so in front of the whole nation.
Does the punishment feel fair? Why tell us this at all, instead of giving Moshe the ending he earned?
10
יְהוֹשֻׁעַ
Entering Israel
~1273 BCE

Yehoshua — Moshe's student — leads the next generation in, and the promise made to Avraham finally pays off. Moshe doesn't get to finish it himself; his student finishes it for him. That's the first link in a chain that never stops: teacher to student, generation to generation.

11
נְבִיאִים וּמְלָכִים
Prophets & Kings
~1000 BCE

The short version: judges first, then kings — Shaul, then David, then Shlomo. David wants to build a permanent home for God's presence, and Shlomo is the one who builds it: the first Temple, in Jerusalem.

Why a building specifically — what does a physical place do that having the Torah alone doesn't?
12
בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ
The Temples
destroyed 70 CE

The first Temple is destroyed. A second one is built in its place. That one is destroyed too — by the Romans, in the year 70. There has been no Temple since.

If the place where you used to feel closest to God is gone, what do you do instead?
13
עֲבוֹדָה
From Korbanot to the Siddur
~350 BCE → 100 CE

Prayer was not invented after the Temple fell as a plan B. It had been running alongside the Temple for centuries already.

  • Ezra and the Anshei Knesset HaGedolah, generations before the destruction, formalize fixed blessing texts and the Amidah — while the Second Temple is still fully operational.
  • The schedule already mirrors the Temple. The twice-daily Tamid offering lines up with Shacharit and Mincha. Musaf exists on Shabbat and holidays because there was an additional korban on exactly those days.
  • 70 CE. As Jerusalem falls, Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai is smuggled out of the city and sets up an academy at Yavneh, so that leadership and teaching don't die with the city. To a despairing student he answers that acts of kindness now do what the offerings did — quoting Hoshea: "I desire kindness, not sacrifice."
  • Rabban Gamliel II, at Yavneh, finalizes the fixed text and order of the Amidah — locked in, in direct response to there being no Temple left.
  • Still visible today. We face Jerusalem when we daven. The Retzei blessing asks God to accept "the fire-offerings of Israel" and to restore the service. Musaf quotes the Torah's sacrifice instructions word for word.
Was prayer a replacement for the Temple, or was it always running in parallel and simply became the whole thing once the building was gone?
14
אֲנַחְנוּ
Landing the Point
your table

Creation, then a family, then a promise, then a nation. A book at Sinai, and a chain of people passing that book down. A building, and offerings twice a day inside it — with a matching prayer schedule running the whole time alongside it. A teacher who got out of a burning city and made sure none of it broke.

And now: us. Still standing at the same times, still facing the same direction, still asking for the same thing to come back.
