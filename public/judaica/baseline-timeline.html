The Baseline Timeline

How did we get from Torah times to today — and what were Torah times actually like?

The whole arc in order, start to finish. Every stop also opens up to show what was going on in the rest of the world at that moment. It ends by handing you off to the Chain of Torah.

Two clocks run down this page The traditional dates come from Seder Olam Rabbah, the rabbinic reckoning the Hebrew calendar counts from — it's why this year is 5786. The historical dates come from archaeology and from records other nations left behind. They line up closely from the Second Temple onward and drift further apart the further back you go; most of the gap is one disagreement, about how long the Persian period lasted. Where they differ, both are shown — the same way section 9 shows three rabbis disagreeing rather than picking one.
1
בְּרֵאשִׁית
Creation
the beginning
traditional
year 1 — 3761 BCE
historical
outside the datable record

God makes the world — light, then sky, then land, then plants and animals. People come at the very end, made on purpose and placed in charge of taking care of everything that came before them.

Why do you think people come last in the story, not first?
You’ve already heard this

Vayechulu — the paragraph sung at Friday night Kiddush, and again on Shabbat morning — is word for word the last few lines of this story, straight out of Bereishit. That tune you already know from the Kiddush cup isn't a poem written about Creation. It's the closing lines of the account itself.

In the world at the time

This is where the Hebrew calendar starts counting, which is why the year 2026 is also the year 5786.

Other peoples in the ancient Near East told creation stories too, and Babylon's — the Enuma Elish — was written down long before ours was. Reading them side by side, the differences are the interesting part. In the Babylonian version the world gets made out of a dead god after a war between gods, and people are built afterward to do the gods' chores. Here there's one God, no fighting, nothing made by accident — and people arrive last on purpose, put in charge rather than put to work.

2
נֹחַ
Noach & the Noachide Laws
covenant #1
traditional
~2104 BCE
historical
no dated event; the story is everywhere

After the flood, God makes a promise — a brit — never to flood the whole earth again. It comes with a short list of basic rules meant for everyone, not just Jews: don't murder, don't steal, treat animals fairly.

If you could only pick a few rules that everybody in the world had to follow, what would you pick?

This is the first promise God makes, and it's addressed to all of humanity. Hold onto the shape of it, because the same thing happens twice more — and each time it gets more specific.

You’ve already heard this

Aleinu — the prayer at the very end of every service, the one where people bow — has a second paragraph most of the room rushes through, and it's explicitly universal: a hope that the whole world, not only Israel, comes to recognize God. That isn't a new idea tacked onto the end of davening. It's this exact promise, the one made to Noach and to everybody after him, still being said out loud at the close of every single service.

In the world at the time

Flood stories turn up all over ancient Mesopotamia, and one of them is startlingly close to ours. In the Epic of Gilgamesh, written down centuries before, a man is warned to build a boat, seals it with pitch, loads his family and animals aboard, and afterward sends out birds to find dry land.

So the shape was familiar to anyone in that world — which makes the changes stand out. In the Gilgamesh version the gods drown everyone because people have gotten too noisy to sleep through, and one god quietly tips off his favorite. Here the flood is a moral judgment, Noach is told plainly why, and it ends with something the other stories don't have: a promise, and a set of rules meant for everyone.

3
אַבְרָהָם
Avraham
covenant #2
traditional
~1813 BCE
historical
Middle Bronze Age — roughly 2000–1550 BCE

Avraham looks around at a world full of idols and works out, on his own, that there's actually one God behind all of it — the first person in the story to really get that. God answers with a new promise, narrower than the last one: this family, specifically, is going to matter, is going to be blessed, and is going to become a nation of its own.

What do you think it felt like to be the only person who believed something everyone else thought was crazy?
In the world at the time

Avraham starts out in Ur, which was a real city and has been dug up — a major place, with a stepped temple tower still standing today in southern Iraq. This is a world of Mesopotamian city-states, writing on clay, long-distance trade, and a great many gods with a great many temples.

To place it: Hammurabi is ruling Babylon and carving his famous law code at roughly this same moment. The Torah's most argued-over neighbor text and its first Jew are near-contemporaries.

One honest wrinkle worth knowing: the story has Avraham's family traveling with camels, and the best evidence we have puts working camels in this region considerably later. People disagree about what to make of that.

4
אָבוֹת
The Patriarchs
three generations
traditional
~1813–1506 BCE
historical
Middle Bronze Age

The promise gets handed down rather than repeated from scratch: Avraham to Yitzchak, Yitzchak to Yaakov, and Yaakov to his twelve sons — who become the twelve tribes.

Why do you think the Torah spends so much time on just one family's story instead of a whole nation right away?
You’ve already heard this

The very first line of the Amidah — the prayer where everyone at shul stands up and goes quiet — names these three, in this order: Elokei Avraham, Elokei Yitzchak, v'Elokei Yaakov. Every time anyone davens the Amidah, anywhere in the world, those three names come first, before anything is asked for. You've watched a room stand silently for that. This is who they're standing for.

In the world at the time

Clay tablets recovered from Bronze Age towns record ordinary family law, and a surprising amount of Bereishit stops looking strange once you read them. Childless couples adopting a servant as heir, then setting that aside when a son is born. Birthrights treated as property that could be traded away. Contracts about which wife's children inherit.

The best example is the oddest scene in the cycle: Rachel taking Lavan's household gods and hiding them under her saddle. In that world those figures could function as the deed to an inheritance — which is why Lavan tears the camp apart over statues he could presumably have replaced.

5
אֶרֶץ כְּנַעַן
The Promise of Canaan
unpaid, for now
traditional
promised ~1813 BCE
historical
Canaan under Egyptian influence

Part of what God promised Avraham was land — this specific place, Canaan, would belong to his descendants. That part of the promise does not get paid off for a very long time.

How many generations do you think pass between the promise and the family actually living there?
In the world at the time

Canaan was not empty land waiting to be occupied. It was a patchwork of walled city-states — Hazor, Megiddo, Shechem — each with its own king, most of them answering in some fashion to Egypt.

We can read their mail. The Amarna letters are a cache of actual diplomatic correspondence from Canaanite rulers to Pharaoh, and they are mostly complaints: rivals are raiding, send troops, the roads aren't safe. It's a useful picture to hold, because it means the promised land was somebody's home, with politics and problems of its own, the entire time the promise was outstanding.

6
יְצִיאַת מִצְרַיִם
Egypt & Passover
slavery → freedom
traditional
~1313 BCE
historical
~1250 BCE — Ramesses II, 19th dynasty

The family grows into a nation while living in Egypt — and then gets enslaved there for generations. God sends Moshe; ten plagues follow; the people go free, and the sea splits on the way out.

We've now had a flood story and a sea-splitting story — what do you think water keeps showing up to represent?
You’ve already heard this

The third paragraph of the Shema ends by naming Yetziat Mitzrayim, the going-out from Egypt, outright. It gets said twice a day, every day — not once a year at the Seder.

You’ve already heard this

And there's a second one, worth its own beat. The moment the sea splits, the people burst into a song — Az Yashir, in Shemot 15. That isn't only a detail in the story: it is said out loud every single morning, in Pesukei D'Zimra, near the start of Shacharit.

You've heard that stretch of the service — sometimes the tune picks up, sometimes the room stands for it. That's this exact song, still being sung, three thousand years later.

In the world at the time

Egypt is at the height of its power, and it doesn't only rule Egypt — it rules Canaan too. That's the detail most people miss: walking out of Egypt toward the promised land did not mean walking out of Egyptian reach.

Semitic-speaking people really did settle in the eastern Nile delta, exactly where the Torah puts them, and one of the store cities named in Shemot — Ra'amses — matches Pi-Ramesses, the real capital Ramesses II built there.

No Egyptian record of the Exodus has been found. That's less damning than it sounds, since Egyptian royal inscriptions were monuments, not journals, and they simply didn't record defeats. What we do have is the Merneptah Stele from about 1208 BCE, an Egyptian victory boast that lists the peoples Pharaoh crushed — and one of the names on it is Israel, already a people, already in Canaan. It's the oldest mention of us outside our own book.

7
מַתַּן תּוֹרָה
Wandering & Sinai
covenant #3
traditional
~1313 BCE
historical
~1250 BCE — Late Bronze Age

At Sinai, God gives the Torah — and this time not to one family, but to the entire nation, all at once, out loud. It's the biggest version yet of the same pattern you've been watching: everyone, then one family, then a whole people — in a real relationship, with real terms.

Why do you think God waited until after they were already free to give them the rules, instead of before?

This is the exact moment the Chain of Torah page picks up — right here, at Sinai. Everything after this point is that same book being handed down, teacher to student, generation to generation, until it reaches us.

You’ve already heard this

Shema — the line you probably half know already, said out loud at shul, sometimes with a tune — isn't just a nice sentence about God being one. It is the actual content of what's being accepted at this exact moment, on this mountain. Every time you say it, you're doing a smaller, quieter version of what an entire nation did right here, out loud, together.

You’ve already heard this

The Aseret HaDibrot — the Ten Commandments themselves — are read publicly in shul with their own special, grander melody, called ta'am elyon, exactly three times a year: the Shabbat of Yitro, the Shabbat of Va'etchanan, and Shavuot. In a great many shuls the whole room stands for that reading in particular.

So if you've ever noticed everyone rise for one specific Torah reading and wondered why that one, there's a good chance this was it — a leftover echo of the entire nation standing at the mountain.

In the world at the time

There's nothing to dig up from a camp in the desert, so archaeology has little to say here directly. But the shape of what happens at Sinai has a striking parallel.

In exactly this era, the Hittite empire wrote treaties between a great king and a people under his protection, and they follow a fixed format: the king names himself, recounts what he has already done for them, sets out the terms, calls witnesses, then lists the blessings for keeping it and the curses for breaking it.

That is, beat for beat, the structure of the covenant at Sinai — right down to opening with "I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt" before a single rule is given. Whatever else you conclude, the covenant is written in the legal language of its own century, and it does something no Hittite treaty does: the whole people, not just the king, hears the terms and agrees to them.

8
מִדְבָּר
The Hard Part
forty years
traditional
1313–1273 BCE
historical
Late Bronze Age Sinai

It does not go smoothly after that. There's a golden calf, and then a moment where the people are too frightened to enter the land they were promised — and are told they'll have to wait. Forty years of wandering follow.

Is it harder to trust a promise before you've seen it come true, or after you've already seen God do the impossible and you still mess up anyway?
In the world at the time

The Sinai wasn't empty wilderness. Egypt ran mining operations there for turquoise and copper, worked in large part by Semitic laborers — and at one of those mining camps, Serabit el-Khadim, something remarkable turned up scratched into the rock.

Those workers had taken Egyptian picture-signs and repurposed them to write their own Semitic language, one sign per sound. It's among the earliest alphabetic writing anyone has found. The alphabet these pages are printed in traces back through Greek and Phoenician to marks like those — made by Semitic workers in the Sinai, in roughly this era.

9
מִשְׁכָּן
The Mishkan
a home that travels
traditional
~1312 BCE — the year after Sinai
historical
Late Bronze Age — a tent leaves nothing to dig up

Right after the golden calf — the worst moment of broken trust in the whole story — God does not walk away. The instruction to Moshe is the opposite: build Me a sanctuary, so that I can dwell among them. It isn't a punishment or a patch. It's a repair, and it moves in the direction nobody would expect: not more distance, but a tent pitched in the middle of the camp.

This is the Mishkan, and it's portable by design — built out of materials the people themselves donated, taken apart and reassembled every single time the nation moves. Inside stand the Aron, the Ark holding the tablets from Sinai, along with a menorah and an incense altar; outside in the courtyard is the altar where the korbanot, the offerings, are brought. Aharon becomes the first Kohen Gadol. And for the first time there is a real, ongoing daily service — offerings every morning and every evening, on a fixed schedule, rather than one-off sacrifices whenever someone feels moved to bring one.

Why would God want a portable house, instead of waiting until they're settled in the land to build something permanent?

One answer worth sitting with: God isn't waiting for them to arrive somewhere safe and finished. God moves in immediately, into a tent, right after their worst failure, and travels with them through the mess rather than meeting them on the other side of it.

Keep this in your pocket That twice-a-day rhythm — a morning offering and an evening one — starts here, at a tent in the desert, decades before there is any Temple at all. It is the thread that eventually becomes Shacharit and Mincha, and it's the reason the prayer schedule in section 14 lands as ancient rather than as a workaround invented after the fact.
In the world at the time

A portable tent-sanctuary sounds improvised until you notice that the ancient world already had one. Egyptian reliefs of Ramesses II's war camp at Kadesh, from roughly this era, show a rectangular enclosure about twice as long as it is wide, entered from the east, with the king's reception tent set toward the back and an innermost chamber beyond it. That is, in outline, the Mishkan's own floor plan — the layout of a great king's field headquarters, borrowed for the God travelling with the camp.

There's a physical trace of the practice too. At Timna, in the Aravah, excavators found a small shrine reused in the twelfth century BCE by Midianites, and around it post holes and heavy dyed woolen cloth — the remains of a tent stretched over a sacred space. Nothing of the Mishkan itself survives, and nobody would expect it to: fabric, poles and skins don't outlast three thousand years. What survives is the evidence that people in exactly this time and place worshipped in tents they could carry.

10
מֵי מְרִיבָה
Moshe's Punishment
the cost
traditional
~1273 BCE — year forty
historical
Kadesh Barnea, northeastern Sinai

At Mei Meriva, God tells Moshe to speak to a rock and it will give water. Instead Moshe loses his temper — "shall we bring water out of this rock for you?" — and hits it. Twice. The water comes anyway, but Moshe is told he will not be the one to lead the people into the land after all.

What was the actual problem — hitting the rock instead of speaking to it, losing his temper, or making it sound like his own power rather than God's? Does it have to be just one?
Three answers Rashi says the miracle got downgraded — a rock that talks is a bigger sign than a rock that's hit. Rambam says the anger itself was the problem. Ramban points at the words: "shall we bring water" made it sound like Moshe and Aharon's own power, and said so in front of the whole nation.
Does the punishment feel fair? Why tell us this at all, instead of giving Moshe the ending he earned?
In the world at the time

Kadesh Barnea is a real oasis in the northeastern Sinai, and the surrounding territories the Torah names — Edom, Moab — are real places with real borders that had to be negotiated or gone around.

Which is worth knowing before deciding the story is about a temper tantrum. In this landscape water was not a convenience, it was the difference between a nation surviving the season and not. Control of a spring was the kind of thing neighboring peoples went to war over. A quarrel about water in that country is a quarrel about whether everyone lives.

11
יְהוֹשֻׁעַ
Entering Israel
the promise paid
traditional
~1273 BCE
historical
~1200 BCE — Iron Age I begins

Yehoshua — Moshe's student — leads the next generation in, and the promise made to Avraham finally pays off. The Mishkan comes with them, still portable, still being taken down and put back up; it moves between several sites over the following centuries before it finally comes to rest in Jerusalem.

Which answers the question left open back at stop 5: how many generations pass between the promise and the payoff? A great many. That gap is most of the Torah, right there. And notice who closes it — Moshe doesn't get to finish this himself, his student does. That's the first link in a chain that never stops: teacher to student, generation to generation.

In the world at the time

This is one of the most dramatic moments in ancient history and it has nothing to do with us. Around 1200 BCE the entire Bronze Age world collapses — within about fifty years the Hittite empire falls, Greek palace civilization ends, trade routes fail, cities burn all over the eastern Mediterranean, and Egypt shrinks back inside its own borders. Displaced peoples move by sea; one group settles the coast and becomes the Philistines.

Into that vacuum, something new shows up in the archaeology: hundreds of small villages appear in the central highlands, unwalled, poor, farming terraces. They have no palaces and no temples — and almost no pig bones, which stands out sharply against every neighboring site. That's the point at which Israel becomes visible in the ground.

How it happened is genuinely argued over: a military conquest, a gradual settling, or people already living in Canaan reorganizing into something new. What isn't argued is that the highland villages are there, and that a people called Israel is in Canaan by 1208 BCE, because Egypt says so on the Merneptah Stele.

12
נְבִיאִים וּמְלָכִים
Prophets & Kings
judges → kings
traditional
David ~877 BCE · Temple built ~832 BCE
historical
David ~1010 BCE · Temple built ~960 BCE

The short version: judges first, then kings — Shaul, then David, then Shlomo. David wants a permanent home for God's presence, in one fixed place rather than a tent that keeps moving, and his son Shlomo is the one who actually builds it: the first Temple, in Jerusalem.

It is essentially the Mishkan's blueprint rendered in stone and gold instead of curtains and poles — same layout, same idea, same twice-daily service. Just built to stay put.

Why a building specifically — what does a fixed, permanent place do that a portable tent doesn't?
In the world at the time

This is where the story starts leaving fingerprints outside itself. In 1993 excavators at Tel Dan found a broken victory monument put up by an enemy king, boasting in Aramaic about defeating the kings of Israel and of the House of David. It dates to roughly 840 BCE — within a couple of centuries of David himself, and the first mention of him anywhere outside the Bible.

What's still argued is the scale. Jerusalem in this period was not a large city by the standards of Egypt or Assyria, and archaeologists disagree sharply about how big and how organized the united kingdom really was. The disagreement is about size, not existence.

Notice too how far apart the two clocks have drifted by now — about 130 years on David alone. That gap keeps widening until the next stop, where it gets resolved.

13
בֵּית הַמִּקְדָּשׁ
The Temples
two buildings, both gone
traditional
First 423 BCE · Second 68 CE
historical
First 586 BCE · Second 70 CE

The first Temple is destroyed. A second one is built in its place. That one is destroyed too — by the Romans, in the year 70. There has been no Temple since.

If the place where you used to feel closest to God is gone, what do you do instead?
In the world at the time

This is where the two clocks split the widest — and then snap back together. The traditional reckoning puts the First Temple's destruction at 423 BCE; Babylonian records, which can be checked against dated astronomical observations, put it at 586 BCE. Nearly the whole difference is one disagreement about how long the Persian empire ruled before Alexander. From the Second Temple onward the two clocks agree.

The first destruction is well documented from the other side: Babylonian chronicles record Nebuchadnezzar taking Jerusalem, and the Lachish letters are actual military dispatches written on pottery during the final weeks, one of them reporting that the signal fires of a neighboring city can no longer be seen.

For the second, the monument is still standing. The Arch of Titus in Rome was built to celebrate the victory, and carved on its inner wall is a parade of Roman soldiers carrying the Temple's menorah away. You can go look at it.

14
עֲבוֹדָה
From Korbanot to the Siddur
the service continues
traditional
Ezra ~350 BCE → Yavneh ~100 CE
historical
Ezra ~450 BCE → Yavneh ~100 CE

Prayer was not invented after the Temple fell as a plan B. It had been running alongside the Temple for centuries already.

  • The rhythm is ancient. The twice-daily offering, morning and evening, didn't begin with either Temple. It began at the Mishkan, in the desert, and then ran unbroken through the first Temple and the second — over a thousand years of the same service, at the same hours, in three different buildings.
  • Ezra and the Anshei Knesset HaGedolah, generations before the destruction, formalize fixed blessing texts and the Amidah — while the Second Temple is still fully operational.
  • The schedule was borrowed on purpose. The Talmud ties the fixed prayer times directly to the Tamid offering — the morning offering to Shacharit, the afternoon one to Mincha. Maariv was added later and stayed slightly optional for a long time, because there was no fixed evening offering to match it to. Musaf exists on Shabbat and holidays because there was an additional korban on exactly those days.
  • 70 CE. As Jerusalem falls, Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai is smuggled out of the city and sets up an academy at Yavneh, so that leadership and teaching don't die with the city. To a despairing student he answers that acts of kindness now do what the offerings did — quoting Hoshea: "I desire kindness, not sacrifice." That isn't a new idea invented on the spot — it's a verse that was already there, now being leaned on.
  • Rabban Gamliel II, at Yavneh, finalizes the fixed text and order of the Amidah — locked in, in direct response to there being no Temple left.
  • Still visible today. We face Jerusalem when we daven. The Retzei blessing asks God to accept "the fire-offerings of Israel" and to restore the service. Musaf quotes the Torah's sacrifice instructions for that day word for word. And Shacharit and Mincha still fall at roughly the hours the Tamid was brought — the clock itself is a fingerprint.
Was prayer a replacement for the Temple, or was it always running in parallel and simply became the whole thing once the building was gone?
You’ve already heard this

Think about Yom Kippur davening — the long stretch of the service where you're standing there and it starts to feel like something is being retold. That isn't a metaphor. The machzor is walking through what the Kohen Gadol actually did in the Temple on that day, step by step, out loud, together, as though the room were watching it happen.

You have already sat through a live reenactment of the Temple service, and almost certainly had no idea that's what it was.

In the world at the time

There's outside evidence for the claim this section makes. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, hidden away before 70 CE, are collections of fixed prayers for set times — written while the Temple was still standing and its offerings were still being brought. Prayer at appointed hours was demonstrably already in use, not invented afterward.

We also have an eyewitness to the fall itself. Josephus was a Jewish commander who ended up in Roman hands and wrote a long account of the war, including the siege and the burning of the Temple. He is not a neutral narrator — he was writing under Roman patronage — but he was there.

And the rebuilding at Yavneh happened against a brutal backdrop. Sixty years later the Bar Kokhba revolt was crushed, Judea was devastated, and Jerusalem was refounded as a Roman city Jews were barred from entering. The academy Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai talked his way out of the city to found is the reason there was still a tradition to carry after all of that.

15
אֲנַחְנוּ
Landing the Point
your table
traditional
5786
historical
2026 — same moment, both clocks

Creation, then a family, then a promise, then a nation. A book at Sinai. A portable tent pitched right after they'd messed up badly, so that God could live close to them anyway. A chain of people passing that book down, and a permanent building raised on that same tent's blueprint. Offerings twice a day running unbroken for over a thousand years across three different buildings, with a matching prayer schedule alongside them the entire time. A teacher who got out of a burning city and made sure none of it broke.

And now: us. Still standing at the same times, still facing the same direction, still asking for the same thing to come back.
